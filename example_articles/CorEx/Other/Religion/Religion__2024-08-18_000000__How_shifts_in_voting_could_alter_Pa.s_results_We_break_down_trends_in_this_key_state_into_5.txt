ABSTRACT
The top of the ballot has changed, but the importance for each party of winning Pennsylvania has not.
All eyes will be on the critical battleground state in the coming months as polls show Vice President Kamala Harris and former President Donald Trump locked in a close race. Voting trends in Pennsylvania provide a road map for the kinds of places that will determine who wins this year's presidential election.
The Inquirer broke up the state into five kinds of places, divided by population density, race, and education, to understand voting changes in recent presidential elections. Each group offers insight into the electorate - and a slight change in any one of them could sway the result this year.
As Pennsylvania's suburbs and prosperous urban areas keep banking more votes for Democrats, rural areas and deep inner cities are turning in the opposite direction. Meanwhile, postindustrial pockets of Pennsylvania, some of which supported Trump in 2016 and then President Joe Biden in 2020, continue to be a wild card.
Together, these crosscutting trends keep the state as competitive as ever. Here are the types of places that could determine which way the political winds will blow in Pennsylvania:
           Rural areas, the state's least dense and most extensive, are also the state's most deeply Republican,
           Suburban areas, the state's most populous, tilt Republican but are becoming increasingly Democratic,
           White working-class urban areas are the closest to 50-50 in the state; Trump won them narrowly in 2016, while Biden barely won them back in 2020,
           White college-educated urban areas are strongly Democratic and growing more so, and
           Non-white urban areas, the state's least populous, are nearly monolithically Democratic.
Of these five groups, the suburbs, white college-educated urban areas, and key white working-class urban areas together brought in hundreds of thousands more votes for Biden in 2020 than they did for Hillary Clinton in 2016. Rural areas netted still more votes for Trump in 2020 than they had in 2016. But heavily blue-majority non-white urban areas also showed weakening enthusiasm for Democrats, in many cases backing Biden by smaller margins than they did Clinton.
Collectively, The Inquirer's analysis shows a shifting electorate: the middle moved left, while turnout changes increased Trump's vote counts at the extremes.
Democrats are hemorrhaging votes in rural areas and inner cities. Education helps explain why.
Central Pennsylvania farmland and racially and ethnically diverse urban areas are culturally very different. But when it comes to voting trends, both have seen lagging or eroding Democratic support.
Rural areas - places that range from Lancaster County dairy farms to the remote forests of Tioga County - are older, whiter, and more Republican than the state as a whole. City neighborhoods where the majority of residents are people of color - such as North Philadelphia, central Reading, and Pittsburgh's Hill District - are younger, more diverse, and overwhelmingly blue. But in 2020, these two types of places shed the most votes for Democrats in Pennsylvania, even as Biden outperformed Clinton statewide.
One trait these places share is that both are home to far fewer residents with college degrees compared with the rest of the state. Since well before Trump ran in 2016, Democrats had been losing ground with people without college degrees. Trump supercharged that trend. And while the narrative about shifts to the right have often focused on the white working class, it's also a phenomenon among working-class Black and Latino voters.
"These places have some commonalities," said David Hopkins, a Boston College political scientist who is writing a book on educational polarization. "They have distrust of the political system. They have this distrust of cultural elites, they have traditionalist predispositions about Christianity, about gender and sexuality."
But voters have drifted away from Democrats for different reasons.
Black and Latino voters are not necessarily flocking to Trump, but are showing ambivalence about the Democratic Party. That presents Harris with an opening to reenergize the Democratic base.
State Rep. Danilo Burgos, a Democrat who represents parts of North Philadelphia, said he largely blames the loss of support on misinformation and a lack of Democratic counter-messaging.
"If you repeat a lie many times over, it becomes the truth," Burgos said of Trump spreading inaccurate information about the economy and crime rates. He said Harris' campaign has been focusing on Spanish-language campaigning, and he predicts a momentum shift in heavily Latino parts of Philadelphia.
Trump has also been working to attract voters of color in cities, rallying in North Philadelphia this summer and opening a campaign office in Reading.
Rural voters, on the other hand, have been solidly Republican for decades and Trump has mobilized them with his populist appeal and social conservatism.
"Anybody who makes things with their hands, they're voting toward the Republican Party," said Sean Logue, who chairs the Republican Party in Washington County, which is about 40% rural, per The Inquirer analysis. "Because the Republican Party's policies are more friendly for energy extraction or advanced manufacturing, whereas Democrats have become the party of giant banks, tech overlords, and Hollywood elites."
Republican registrations have increased in Washington County, which Trump won in 2020 with 61% of the vote. Logue expects to see an even larger margin of victory in November.
Suburbs and educated white city neighborhoods have become Democrats' ace in the hole.
Voters in areas such as Philadelphia's University City and the Philadelphia collar counties represent the growing base the Democratic Party has relied on in recent presidential elections.
Affluent and majority-white suburban areas statewide are still majority Republican. But Democrats have expanded their numbers rapidly.
The ideology of those areas has shifted, and suburban and wealthy urban areas are banking more Democratic votes in part because they also have the fastest growing populations.
Take Cumberland County, where a booming suburban population outside of Harrisburg has juiced Democratic numbers even as the county remains Republican overall.
And the solidly Democratic counties keep getting bluer.
"Delaware County has proven time and time again, especially since 2016, that we get out the vote. ... I just see that continuing," said Monica Taylor, who chairs the Delaware County Council and sits on the advisory board for Harris' campaign in the state. Voters are motivated by reproductive rights, fears of a second Trump term, and the historic nature of Harris' candidacy, Taylor said.
Delaware County, like many suburban areas, encompasses a diverse swath of voters. Education levels also correlate with how much suburban voters have shifted toward Democrats; suburbs where fewer residents are college-educated were less likely to have moved left between 2016 and 2020.
But areas with higher educational attainment are still growing for Democrats, due in part to moderate voters who were critical to Biden's 2020 victory. Which direction they go could be key this year, too. Craig Snyder, who lives in Philadelphia and leads a group focused on getting Republican supporters of former South Carolina Gov. Nikki Haley to back Harris in November, said those moderate voters represent Democrats' best hope of expanding their base.
"The only place [for Democrats] to grow is college-educated, center-right voters who might have Republican DNA imprinting but who don't like the party of Donald Trump," he said.
Where will the white working class go?
The Pennsylvania voting bloc that remains most in flux is working-class residents of Pennsylvania's once-mighty manufacturing and mining cities.
These largely blue-collar places - such as Johnstown, Altoona, Wilkes-Barre, and Marcus Hook - were longtime Democratic strongholds. But Trump won many of them in 2016.
Biden, native son of Scranton, was able to draw some of these voters back in 2020. But not to pre-2016 levels.
Gene Ziemba, a retired insurance agent, leads the Luzerne County Republicans and has roots in Nanticoke, a former coal town where his grandfather was head of the United Mine Workers. Even generations after mines, steel mills, and factories were shut down, he said, families still feel lingering economic distress and a frustration that Democrats have left them behind.
"This is really the score, right there," Ziemba said.
Of the three Pennsylvania counties that swung from President Barack Obama in 2012 to Trump in 2016, only Luzerne stuck with Trump in 2020. Once a more Democratic county, it's now evenly divided between registered Republicans and Democrats.
Ziemba thinks neighboring Lackawanna County, home to Scranton, went blue in 2020 because of Biden's roots there. Without Biden on the ballot, he expects bigger numbers for Trump in the region.
"The inflation alone is doing it," Ziemba said. "People have squirreled away, saved money for years, and even if their numbers say they have more money, they have less buying power."
These voters are such a key bloc that both Trump and Harris picked running mates who they think can appeal to them. U.S. Sen. JD Vance of Ohio will reportedly spend significant time in Pennsylvania.
"When you look at his life experience, where he grew up, how he grew up, I think that will translate to younger voters who can relate to him, especially in times like this," said Pennsylvania House Minority Leader Bryan Cutler (R., Lancaster).
And Democrats hope Minnesota Gov. Tim Walz could have appeal in those same parts of the state.
"As Pennsylvania voters get to know him, he's a thoroughly wholesome, Midwest guy who was a veteran and a teacher," Harris senior adviser Brendan McPhillips said. "That plays in Pennsylvania."
That was then. What about now?
It's possible these trends will waver in this year's presidential race, but it's unlikely they'll change dramatically.
"The divide that's emerged remains in place," said Chris Borick, director of the Institute of Public Opinion at Muhlenberg College. "Among highly educated suburban folks, there's disgust with the Trump takeover of the Republicans. And even with the replacement of candidates, there's a major disconnect between Democrats and the groups who are attracted to Trump's populism."
The state's highly coveted 19 electoral votes could be won by a slim margin. In both 2016 and 2020, the presidential race in Pennsylvania was determined by tens of thousands of votes.
And both campaigns know this well. Trump proclaimed the commonwealth "one of the most important battlegrounds" at a February rally in Harrisburg. And Democratic Sen. John Fetterman told Harris supporters this month in Philadelphia:
"Pennsylvania picks the presidency."
